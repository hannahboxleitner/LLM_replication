Using (open) LLMs for linguistic annotation tasks: Replication of a study by Morin & Marttinen Larsson (2025)

Author

Hannah Boxleitner

Introduction

In the last decades, researchers in corpus linguistics have gained access to increasingly massive corpora of natural language in different languages. This poses great opportunities, but also necessitates manual cleaning and processing of large amounts of data (Morin and Larsson 2025: 1). The rise of large language models (LLMs) is therefore a rather interesting development for corpus linguistic research, making it possible to handle larger datasets and corpora automatically. In recent years, a rising number of papers testing LLMs for assisting in linguistic research have been published (sources).

I aim to replicate a study by Morin and Larsson (2025), which presents a replicable method for leveraging LLMs for assistance in linguistic grammatical annotation. While the study was conducted with the model Claude Sonnet 3.5, I will attempt to replicate their methodological pipeline using the same data, but with two open LLMs in R.

This project aims to embody the principles of open science in two respects: First, I want to support the use and examination of open LLMs for analysis of linguistic data. While closed LLMs are now commonly used in day-to-day life, their usage brings difficulties regarding data rights and privacy, (see presentation for citation) among others. Many open alternatives are on the rise, but seem to be not as widely used and investigated in studies as necessary (source?). Generalizability considering open models is therefore a relevant concern, especially in relation to open science. Second, I want to conduct a replication study and thereby contribute to the central aim of making linguistic research more cumulative and robust. The study was pre-registered before conducting the analysis at OSF (Boxleitner 2026). ??

Planning the replication

Deciding on a type of replication

close replication? (Röseler et al. 2025: Chapter 4) In planning and defining the terms for this project, I encountered some problems with the terminology of replication research. Many things that need to be considered for reproductions or replications are usually formulated with respect to experiments with participants, where sample sizes etc. matter. Replications are also often defined as repeating a study with different data, for example (source: table in Röseler). For this project, a replication of a study that leverages LLMs to assist in grammatical annotation for linguistic research, a lot of the terminology does not fit or apply. While I intend to use the same data as Morin and Larsson (2025), which I intend to replicate with another LLM, one can argue that using different models creates different data. Also, the models somehow represent participants in this setting, since they basically replace annotators who would otherwise do this task manually. maybe aufgreifen in the conclusion! entweder hier noch auf data leakage eingehen oder später Therefore, I had to adapt the theoretical framework as possible, and go from there. Since I plan on replicating Morin and Larsson (2025)’s approach very closely, use the data that is provided, and also adapt their prompting very closely, this project would probably be considered a close replication. Achieving a very close resemblance with the original study can be difficult for multiple reasons. Among others, original studies may not include sufficient detail in some respects (Röseler et al. 2025: Chapter 6.3). In the case of replicating Morin and Larsson (2025), the authors share their data and a transcript of the conversation, while also explaining their approach in the paper, but the code with which the automated pipeline was implemented (or even the programming language, for example) is not shared. This is an important aspect to consider, since resembling the study in terms of implementing the pipeline itself is only possible to a limited extent.

Planning the methodological pipeline

To plan my replication, I read the study by Morin and Larsson (2025) thoroughly, especially focusing on the pre-training and prompt engineering.

Further insight could be gained by going through the conversation transcript that Morin and Larsson (2025) provided with their data. It lists user prompts and answers of the model in a docx.-file. The conversation consists of the following parts:

Pretraining

  • First, the LLM gets a user prompt containing detailed instructions on how to classify the sentences, and is presented with examples for every instance and a step-by-step approach to classification.

  • It is instructed to look at 493 pretraining sentences in a dataset containing correct classifications, think about each classification step-by-step, and encouraged to ask questions.

Training

  • A round of supervised classifications follows: The model is prompted to classify the first 100 sentences (dataset: supervised_training_sketchengine_100.csv) in batches of 25. Mistakes are corrected through user prompts directly.

  • Unsupervised training round 1: After that, another dataset of 100 sentences is requested to be classified without the close supervision (and to be provided in tab-separated .csv-format).

  • All incorrect classifications are pasted into the next user prompt. The model is told its accuracy for the task and instructed to review the errors. After that, the reviews are not further commented by the authors, the model is simply told to keep those lessons in mind.

  • Unsupervised training round 2: It is requested to classify step-by-step and consistently apply the insertion test, then given new data (EnTenTen_random_sample_101.csv), and told to do the full classification in one batch in .csv format.

  • It is told the accuracy rate (93%) and told that it is good

Testing

  • Provided with new data (EnTenTen_random_sample_102.csv) and told to follow the procedure that was successfully applied in the latest round of classifications. After classification, it is again told the accuracy.

All of these steps were incorporated in the replication. I created a setup script that includes the API call and the basis for a multi-turn conversation with the LLM. The analyses (one with each open LLM) were then conducted in separate scripts.

When looking at the datasets that Morin and Larsson (2025) provided in their repository, there were some aspects I needed to consider. For example, the file naming was not very intuitive or informative regarding the methodological pipeline. For some training or testing rounds, the classifications done by Claude or the human annotator were provided in different files, for others, there was only one file containing everything. Also, the column names varied throughout he files (for example, “Sentence” vs. “Consider-construction”, or “Claude_classification” vs. “Classification_Claude”). While this would call for some data cleaning before running the analyses, all of these concerns were handled programmatically and only changed in the R environment, while the authors’ files remained unchanged.

Piloting

Since the methodological approach proposed by Morin and Larsson (2025) is quite extensive and detailed, and there is no code to look into for aspects like e.g. how the datasets were implemented in the prompts, I decided to conduct a little pilot study before the actual replication, to test a similar multi-turn-conversation. This script was run and altered several times in preparation for the analysis.

I used a small dataset I had annotated manually once for another project. The task was a sentiment analysis with a lot less detailed instructions. The goal of this pilot was not to follow Morin and Larsson (2025)’s approach exactly or to achieve a high accuracy, but rather to implement the steps of pretraining, training and testing and make sure the model can access the datasets. In the last round of testing, I actually overlooked one step of informing the model about the “new” accuracy of corrections, which might have improved accuracy in the end. This made me further aware of the exact process and glad I conducted a pilot study.

LLMs

The aim of this project is to replicate the study by Morin and Larsson (2025) with different, and especially open LLMs. The authors used Claude Sonnet 3.5, an LLM that is known for strong performance and often compared with ChatGPT, including in NLP studies (Morin and Larsson 2025: 3).

The first model chosen for this project is Mistral Small 3.2 24B Instruct 2506, which was released in 2025 under Apache 2.0 license. It is a minor update of Mistral-Small-3.1-24B-Instruct-2503, improved in the area of instruction following, repetition errors, and function calling.

(second source!). Arguably, open weights are the most important aspect for reproducibility (Nils Reiter 2025: 23). While Morin and Larsson (2025) used a rather well-known, commercial, and closed model for their study, I want to compare this with the performance of two open ones. Mistral … is a smaller, European model, while GPT OSS .. is an open version of ChatGPT (Ableger) and way bigger. Seeing how these two perform in comparison to the model tested in Morin and Larsson (2025) on a linguistic annotation task is not just of interest for this project, but also relevant for research on LLM usage in linguistics in general.

Considering the concern of data leakage, it is important to note that the Mistral model was released in June 2025, and the GPT model in … Morin and Larsson (2025), who used data from the NOW corpus and Sketch Engine’s EnTenTen21 corpus for their study (Morin and Larsson 2025: 3), created their OSF-database also containing the annotated datasets used as the gold standard for comparison, in November 2024. When and on which data the model was trained exactly is unclear, but there were definitely changes and fine-tuning on the Mistral model after the repository went public.

Replication criteria and hypotheses

Morin and Larsson (2025) present a methodological pipeline leveraging an LLM for the automatic annotation of grammatical data, based on the case study of the evaluative consider construction in English. Their research questions are concerned with the frequency of the different variants of the consider construction. While they don’t formulate a hypothesis in their paper, they state a twofold goal to 1) build a replicable methodological pipeline that can be repurposed and 2) to substantially improve the quality of annotation of future corpus studies (morin2025:3?).

Keeping these concerns about the generalizability of the author’s approach in mind, the present study addresses the following research question:Can the methodological pipeline proposed by Morin and Larsson (2025) be successfully replicated using a different large language model on the same linguistic classification task?

To answer this research question, replicability needs to be measurable in this context. The authors state in their abstract to have reached “a model accuracy of over 90%” (Morin and Larsson 2025: 1), and lastly achieved an accuracy rate of 93% with their model. As one can see in the conversation transcript, the user prompt contains a desired accuracy level of 95% to illustrate to the model what constitutes a successful task completion.

For this project, a successful replication is defined as achieving an accuracy ≥ 90% using the original dataset and methodological pipeline proposed by Morin and Larsson (2025).

While attempting to reach this goal, the focus of this project - with regard to principles of open science - remains the replication itself, which, as mentioned before, is supposed to be as close as possible to the methodological pipeline presented in Morin and Larsson (2025), including specifics like

  • using XML-tags in user prompts (e.g. for <instructions> classification instructions </instructions>)

  • putting all the instruction inside user prompts (as suggested in the transcript) as opposed to implementing a system prompt

  • and so on.

Based on these considerations, I want to establish the following hypothesis for this project: Using the methodological pipeline and dataset from Morin and Larsson (2025), the open model Mistral Small 3.2 24B Instruct 2506 will achieve an accuracy of at least 90% on the linguistic classification task when evaluated against the human gold standard.

As a secondary component of my analysis, inter-rater agreement was evaluated. Overall agreement across all annotators (Model 1, Model 2, and the human gold standard provided in the study data) was assessed using Fleiss’ Kappa, and pairwise comparisons (every model against the human gold standard) using Cohen’s Kappa.

e.g. Fleiss’ Kappa (see Larsson et al.) -> accounting for accidental agreement seems especially relevant in terms of LLMs, because there would be no real way of knowing if the models would simply guess.

Inter-rater agreement (also referred to as inter-annotator agreement, or inter-rater reliability) is often measured for purposes like improving annotation schemes or identifying difficulties in the source (Artstein 2017: 298), which is why it can be done at different stages of a study, e.g. before conducting the full-scale annotation.

Considering reproducibility

In the study I run, there is no inherent form of randomization present. Also, reproducibility in LLM studies can be tricky, especially if the models are run remotely via an API. But, making the study and the results as reproducible as possible, is still an important concern, and there are things that can be controlled.

To enhance reproducibility, I fixed the generation parameters in the API call by setting temperature to 0, topp to 1, and using a constant random seed. Setting temperature to 0, meaning the model always chooses the most probable next token, removes random sampling and therefore makes additional constraints such as topk (sampling only from the k most probable tokens) unnecessary and potentially confounding. The number used as a random seed was derived from lotto numbers drawn on March 25, 2026, which had not yet been disclosed at the time of pre-registration.

Even though these measures were taken to improve reproducibility, identical outputs across runs cannot be guaranteed, as some sources of variability within the API remain outside my control.

Another thing that has to be mentioned here is the concern of data leakage. For my study, it is assumed that the models, functioning as several “annotators”, independently label the data without knowledge of each other’s work. It can only be assumed because there is a possibility that the models I used might have the gold standard provided in the original study as part of their training data, which would mean they would not be totally independent.

Inter-rater agreement

Aside from measuring the success of the replication with a desired level of accuracy, I also want to analyze inter-rater agreement. While a straightforward way to do this would be to count the number of items for which annotators provide identical labels, and report that number as a percentage of the total (so-called raw or observed agreement), accidental agreement is not accounted for in this approach, which impacts reliability (and reproducibility). Instead, I want to utilize a coefficient from the kappa/alpha family: This is considered to measure a more meaningful agreement through involving agreement by chance, resulting in a coefficient whose value is 1 when agreement is perfect, and 0 when agreement is at chance level (Artstein 2017: 299-300). Since with LLMs it seems every more likely that some amount of agreement is by chance, I think it important to account for this issue in measuring inter-rater agreement. (does this have to be in hypothesis part? and here only the results of the analysis)

First, calculate Fleiss’ kappa: suitable for comparing more than two annotators (Mistral, Claude, and human gold standard), and suitable when there are only two categories/labels (Artstein 2017: 300).

In a second part, I want to take into consideration Cohen’s kappa, used to measure agreement between two raters. -> for pairwise comparison (Claude and HGS; Mistral and HGS). -> the purpose of including this measurement is to draw conclsuions about which model worked “better” for annotation (compared to the HGS).

Discussion of outcomes in light of differences

is the approach indeed replicable? -> things like: there is no code or any information on how the pipeline was actually implemented and automated. That would have been really helpful, or would be helpful for further studies. Also, looking at the trnascript, some things were not really consistent. For example, while instructions were supposed to always be given in XML-tags, this was not used in between (e.g. “next batch” etc., see transcript).

For the unsupervised training, Morin and Larsson (2025) ran two rounds. In their paper it is mentioned that this “iterative training and validation cycle was repeated until Claude achieved the desired level of accuracy, with each round of classifications always providing unseen data” (Morin and Larsson 2025: 6). Since I wanted to replicate this study with the exact same data, I could not exactly follow this part, as I would have stopped after the second round of unsupervised training, even if the model would not have reached a high accuracy level.
